In the Highway 4 and Highway 49 corridors, cell coverage is uneven on an ordinary afternoon. Add a public-safety power shutoff, or a busy fire day when half the county reaches for a phone at once, and the bars you were counting on can thin out or drop. The plan that helps most on that day is the one your household wrote down before it.

September is [National Preparedness Month](https://www.ready.gov/september), and of all the readiness chores it prompts, the cheapest and most overlooked is a written household communication plan — an agreement, made ahead of time, for how your family reaches each other and where you regroup when the usual channels are busy or down. It costs nothing and takes an evening.

A few pieces do most of the work.

**An out-of-area contact.** Pick one person who lives well outside the foothills — a relative in another state is ideal — and make them everyone's check-in point. During a local emergency, calls across town compete for the same loaded circuits, while [a call or text to someone out of the area is often easier to complete](https://www.ready.gov/plan). Each family member reports "I'm okay, I'm here" to that one contact, who relays it around. It turns a dozen failing local calls into one that stands a better chance.

**Text, don't call.** When networks are saturated, [a text message may go through when a voice call will not](https://www.fcc.gov/reports-research/guides/tips-communicating-emergency) — short data messages slip through gaps a held-open voice channel can't. Texting also leaves voice capacity for people who need a live line. If you must call, keep it brief, and space out your attempts rather than redialing in a rush, which only adds to the congestion.

**Put it on paper.** A plan that lives only in a dead phone is no plan. Write the contact numbers and the meeting spots on a card for each wallet, backpack, and glovebox, and keep a copy in the phone too. Ready.gov has a [fill-in wallet card](https://www.ready.gov/plan-form) if you'd rather not start from a blank page.

**Name where you regroup.** Decide two places to meet if you can't reach each other — one near home, one outside the immediate area — so "we couldn't get through" doesn't also become "we don't know where you went."

**A channel that skips the phone network.** A pair of handheld radios gives a household a way to talk that never touches a cell tower. The [FRS radios](https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/family-radio-service-frs) sold in blister packs need no license and work out of the box for short range; a [GMRS license](https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/general-mobile-radio-service-gmrs) — no exam, and once one adult holds it every family member is covered under the single callsign — adds power and range on the same band for a flat [$35 over ten years](https://www.fcc.gov/personal-service-and-amateur-application-fees). Agree on a channel now, while you can read the manual, not while the lights are off.

A household plan costs almost nothing and takes one evening. Pick the contact, print the cards, name the meeting spots, and set the channel this month. The plan is already in place before the dry weeks give way to the first shutoff.
