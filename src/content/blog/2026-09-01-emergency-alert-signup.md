In the Highway 4 and Highway 49 corridors, word that a fire has jumped a ridge or a road is closing can arrive two very different ways — and only one of them finds you with no effort on your part. Sorting out which alerts actually reach your phone is a good chore for [National Preparedness Month](https://www.ready.gov/september), and it costs nothing but an evening.

## The alert nobody signs up for

The message that buzzes every phone at once — an evacuation warning, a flash-flood alert — is a Wireless Emergency Alert (WEA). [WEA is not a subscription](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/wireless-emergency-alerts): there is nothing to sign up for and no personal information to hand over. Authorized officials send it through a federal system, and [cell towers in the targeted area broadcast it to every capable phone connected to them](https://www.weather.gov/riw/WEA_Info). Because it rides a broadcast channel rather than ordinary texting, it gets through when the network is congested — the busy-day problem that stalls a normal call.

The catch is in that last phrase: connected to them. A WEA reaches a phone that is within range of a cell tower carrying the alert. In a canyon, or a stretch of the corridor with no bars, there is no tower to broadcast it, and the alert that buzzes every phone in town never arrives at the cabin up the draw. The automatic channel is only as wide as cell coverage — which, in this terrain, is not everywhere.

## The alert you have to ask for

That gap is what the county systems are for. Calaveras County runs [Calaveras Alert](https://fireprevention.calaverasgov.us/Emergency-Alerts), and Tuolumne County runs [its own alert system](https://www.tuolumnecounty.ca.gov/1170/Emergency-Alerts) — both built on the Everbridge platform, both operated by each county's Office of Emergency Services. Register once and the county can reach the numbers and addresses you give it — cell, landline, email, text — with the evacuation and safety notices tied to where you live.

The difference that matters: these are opt-in. Nobody is enrolled automatically. Registration takes a few minutes on the county's page, and it is worth entering every address you would want covered — home, a parent's place, the property you would drive to.

One more reason to check rather than assume you are covered: alerting systems change hands. A [ransomware attack knocked the widely used CodeRED platform offline in November 2025](https://www.securityweek.com/ransomware-attack-disrupts-local-emergency-alert-system-across-us/), and some counties dropped it and moved on afterward. A registration you made years ago, on a system your county may no longer run, is not a live subscription. Re-confirming takes less time than it took to read this.

## Where the orders themselves live

Signing up controls how a warning reaches you; it does not change who issues it. Evacuation orders come from the sheriff and are mapped by zone — the foothills use [Genasys](https://protect.genasys.com) — and fire-weather warnings come from [NWS Sacramento](https://www.weather.gov/sto). A county alert puts those in your hand faster, and the [Live Feed](/live) mirrors them, but the agencies remain the source.

Pick an evening this month: register with your county's system, add every address that matters, and confirm the contact details on file are current. The next time a warning goes out, whether it reaches you is a question you will have already answered.
