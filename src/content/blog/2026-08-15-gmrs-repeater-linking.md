For a lot of foothill households, a General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS) radio is the simple fallback for when the cell bars and the power go at the same time — a licensed two-way band that works handset to handset with no tower in the path. A recent federal enforcement action is a useful reminder of one boundary on what a GMRS repeater is allowed to do, and why that boundary sits where it does.

## The action

On August 10 the FCC's Enforcement Bureau issued a [Notice of Violation](http://www.arrl.org/news/fcc-cites-gmrs-licensee-for-illegal-linked-repeater-network) to the head of a Georgia GMRS group whose system had grown to nearly two dozen repeaters connected to one another over the internet. Agents had observed in February that the linked repeaters were relaying a single message across all of them for simultaneous transmission over a wide area. That, the notice says, violates [Section 95.1749](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/95.1749) of the FCC's rules. The licensee was given 20 days to respond.

The rule itself is short. A GMRS repeater "may be connected to the public switched network or other networks" — but only "for the sole purpose of operation by remote control." Wiring repeaters together to pass traffic between them, so a conversation carries past the range of the radios actually in it, is not remote control. It is linking, and for GMRS it is not permitted.

## Why the line is drawn there

The reason is spectrum, not caprice. GMRS shares eight repeater channel pairs nationwide among more than 300,000 licensees, the FCC's notice notes. On a standalone repeater those channels are reused constantly — a canyon in California and a valley in Georgia hold entirely separate conversations on the same pair, because neither hears the other. Link a chain of repeaters together and one conversation occupies the channel across every place the chain reaches, crowding out everyone else on a resource there is very little of.

Amateur ("ham") radio, which requires an exam and is allotted far more spectrum, does permit repeater linking. GMRS, which asks only a [$35 license](https://www.fcc.gov/personal-service-and-amateur-application-fees) and no test, keeps each repeater local instead. That is the trade that comes with the low doorway in.

## What it means for a home plan

For anyone weighing GMRS as part of a household or neighborhood communications plan, the practical read is straightforward. A single well-sited repeater covering a watershed is exactly what the service is built for, and it is legal. A wide-area, internet-linked GMRS "network" advertised as reaching across counties is not — and a plan that leans on one is leaning on something the FCC can order shut down. If you join a local net, it is worth knowing whether the repeater you reach is a standalone machine on a ridgeline or a node in an internet-linked chain. The first is GMRS as designed.

Those eight shared channels are the whole reason for the rule. Keeping each repeater local is what lets them carry hundreds of thousands of separate local conversations at once — the ordinary, license-light service a foothill household is actually reaching for.
