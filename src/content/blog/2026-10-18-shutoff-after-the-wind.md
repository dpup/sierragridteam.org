A public safety power shutoff is built around a wind forecast, but the outage it produces outlasts the wind by a wide margin. This month's shutoff across the Calaveras and Tuolumne foothills is a clean example — and the stretch after the wind died is the part worth planning for.

## What happened

PG&E began cutting power the evening of Wednesday, October 14, ahead of a dry, windy system. By Friday morning, when the last circuits were de-energized, [roughly 85,900 customers across 29 counties](https://www.pge.com/en/newsroom/currents/safety/pge-may-need-to-turn-off-power-for-safety.html) had lost power. Calaveras and Tuolumne were among the hardest hit: roughly 10,790 customers out in Calaveras and 7,248 in Tuolumne. PG&E opened [more than 40 Community Resource Centers](https://kioncentralcoast.com/news/2026/10/13/pge-eyes-another-northern-california-power-shutoff-after-weekend-psps/) — power, water, and device charging — across the affected counties.

The wind itself was brief. [PG&E's final update came the evening of Saturday, October 17](https://www.pge.com/en/newsroom/currents/safety/pge-may-need-to-turn-off-power-for-safety.html), reporting virtually all customers restored. In the two counties, the lines went dark Wednesday evening and power came back over the following two days.

## Why the lights stayed off

Restoration does not begin when the wind drops. It begins when crews can safely reach the lines. Once an area is declared "all clear," [PG&E patrols nearly every de-energized line — by air, by vehicle, and on foot](https://www.turnto23.com/news/state/pg-e-to-begin-patrolling-power-lines-amid-psps-events) — checking for branches, damaged equipment, or other hazards the wind left behind. Only lines inspected and found clear are switched back on; where a crew finds damage, that section waits for repair while the rest of the circuit comes up.

That inspection is slow by design, and it runs in daylight. A single wind event can leave thousands of line-miles to walk and fly before a switch is thrown. So a shutoff is really two events stacked end to end: the weather, over in hours, and the restoration, which trails it by a day or more.

## Planning for the gap

For anyone who keeps a radio for the days the grid is down, the lesson is to size the plan to the full outage, not the first night. A two-day power loss is also a two-day communications problem. Phones need charging they can no longer draw from the wall — and the cell network itself is not a sure thing through a long event. Cell sites run on commercial power with limited battery backup; [the FCC does not require carriers to keep them powered](https://www.khsu.org/regional-interests/2019-11-06/heres-why-you-lost-cell-service-during-the-pg-e-power-shutoffs), and in past shutoffs towers went dark as their batteries drained.

A few things hold up across a multi-day outage, and all of them want doing before one starts:

- **Charge ahead.** Power banks and a vehicle charger keep a phone and a handheld alive into the restoration tail, not just the first evening.
- **Keep a radio that needs no grid.** A battery or hand-crank NOAA weather radio receives the National Weather Service broadcast directly — no cell site, no account, no power. It is one channel that does not depend on the lines being up.
- **Read restoration times as estimates.** PG&E's posted return times are projections it revises as crews work; use them to plan, not to count on a particular hour.

Most of this month's outage came after the weather had already moved on. The preparation that matters for it — charging, a grid-independent radio, a realistic read on restoration times — all has to be in place before the lines go down.
