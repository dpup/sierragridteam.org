Handheld two-way radios are a common backup when cell service is marginal or the power is out. A federal rule that took effect this month changes how those radios are sold online. The rule leaves gaps, so the buyer should check what a radio is certified to do before buying it.

## The new marketplace rule

In July the FCC issued an order that makes online marketplaces share legal responsibility for keeping unauthorized radio devices off their sites, [ARRL reports](https://www.arrl.org/news/ebay-restricts-listings-of-radio-equipment-but-exempts-ham-band-only-gear). The rules took effect October 13. That day eBay began asking sellers for a valid FCC ID on applicable listings, and [told them](https://www.valueaddedresource.net/ebay-fcc-id/) that listings missing it "may be hidden from buyers in the US." Its list of affected products includes two-way radios.

The rule does not reach every listing. Listings already online, listings from small sellers, and used equipment are exempt, according to ARRL, and the FCC did not change its underlying equipment authorization rules. A listing with no FCC ID, particularly for a used radio, tells a buyer nothing about whether the radio is certified.

## What an FCC ID is

FCC rules require certified equipment to [bear a label](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/2.925) showing its identifier after the words "FCC ID." The identifier has [two parts](https://wiki.radioreference.com/index.php/FCC_ID): a grantee code that identifies the company, and a product code the company assigns. Entering the full ID in the FCC's [FCC ID search](https://www.fcc.gov/oet/ea/fccid) brings up the equipment grant, which lists the rule parts and frequency ranges the radio is approved for.

## Matching the radio to the service

Consumer handhelds sold for household use generally fall under Part 95 of the FCC's rules. Family Radio Service (FRS) equipment is certified under subpart B and General Mobile Radio Service (GMRS) equipment under [subpart E](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/95.1761), so the rule part on a grant reads 95B or 95E.

That certification matters for the operator as well as the seller. The rule says ["no person shall operate a transmitter in any Personal Radio Service unless it is a certified transmitter"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/47/95.335) for that service, and that using one that is not certified "voids the user's authority to operate that station." A GMRS license does not cover an uncertified radio.

The inexpensive dual-band handhelds sold to new amateur radio operators are a separate case. A radio that can transmit only on amateur bands needs no FCC certification, and a licensed amateur may operate it under their own license, according to the FCC's [2018 enforcement advisory](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-18-980A1.pdf). The same advisory notes that "many two-way radios that purport to operate on amateur frequencies also operate on frequencies that extend beyond the designated amateur frequency bands," and a radio like that may not be sold or used in the US without certification. Unless its grant lists 95E, such a radio is not certified for GMRS, whatever channels it can be programmed to reach.

## Before you buy

- Find the FCC ID on the radio's label, its packaging, or its manual. Some radios show it on a settings screen instead.
- Look the ID up and confirm the grant lists the service you plan to use: 95B for FRS, 95E for GMRS.
- Compare the ID on the radio with the one in the listing. eBay asks sellers to make sure the ID "applies to the exact product being listed."
- Expect no FCC ID on a radio built only for the amateur bands. It is meant for licensed operators on amateur frequencies.
