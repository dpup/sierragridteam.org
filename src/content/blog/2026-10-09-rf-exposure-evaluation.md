Plenty of foothill households keep a radio for the days the grid and the cell network are down, and a fair number of neighbors hold an amateur — "ham" — license and run a base station from the property. Since 2021, federal rules have required every one of those stations to be checked for radiofrequency exposure. A pair of federal proceedings opened in September is a good prompt to confirm yours has been.

## What the rule asks

An RF exposure evaluation is a way of determining whether the energy a transmitting antenna puts into the space where people spend time stays within the FCC's exposure limits. The limits are not new. What changed is who has to check. Before, most amateur stations were categorically excluded — below a certain power, an operator could skip the assessment. The FCC removed that blanket exclusion for amateur radio, and the new rules [took effect May 3, 2021](https://www.arrl.org/news/updated-radio-frequency-exposure-rules-become-effective-on-may-3), with stations already on the air given [until May 3, 2023](https://www.arrl.org/news/updated-radio-frequency-exposure-rules-become-effective-on-may-3) to finish an evaluation. Both dates have passed.

The exposure limits themselves did not move. ARRL is explicit that the change ["does not change existing RF exposure (RFE) limits"](https://www.arrl.org/news/updated-radio-frequency-exposure-rules-become-effective-on-may-3) — it ends the shortcut that let a low-power station skip the step. Every amateur now has to work out whether their station [qualifies for an exemption or needs a routine evaluation](https://www.arrl.org/news/updated-radio-frequency-exposure-rules-become-effective-on-may-3).

## Why it is in the news again

On September 17, 2026, the FCC and the Department of Health and Human Services [opened parallel comment periods](https://www.arrl.org/news/fcc-and-hhs-initiate-new-proceedings-on-rf-exposure) on RF safety — the FCC on exposure questions a federal appeals court sent back to it in 2021, HHS on broader health research. ARRL's read is that the inquiries ["do not change the current FCC RF exposure rules"](https://www.arrl.org/news/fcc-and-hhs-initiate-new-proceedings-on-rf-exposure); they could inform changes in a later proceeding. Comments were due October 21 (HHS) and October 22 (FCC). The standing requirement is unchanged: if your station transmits, it still needs to have been evaluated.

## How you actually evaluate a station

This does not take a meter or a consultant. The evaluation is a calculation from a few things an operator already knows: the band, the power reaching the antenna, the antenna's gain, and how far the nearest occupied space sits from it. ARRL publishes a free [RF Exposure Calculator](https://www.arrl.org/rf-exposure-calculator); its [RF exposure resources](https://www.arrl.org/rf-exposure) — the calculator, a handbook chapter, a short video, and an FAQ — are publicly available. Run the numbers, and keep the result on file; the FCC can ask to see it.

## The foothill wrinkle

Height and a clear line of sight beat raw wattage in this terrain — a ridgeline antenna reaches where a handheld down in a canyon cannot, which is why operators here work to get an antenna up and in the clear. The same factors drive the exposure math. A higher-gain antenna, more transmit power, or a feedpoint close to a deck, window, or walkway all push a station toward the full evaluation rather than the exemption. If the summer project was getting an antenna above the treeline, the evaluation is the companion task that finishes it.

Running the numbers takes an evening, and the calculator does the arithmetic. Do it once for the station as it stands, note the result, and redo it whenever the antenna, the power, or the layout changes.
