The time to find out a repeater is dead, or that half the operators have forgotten the net frequency, is a quiet Saturday in October — not the afternoon a fire crosses a ridge above the corridor. That is the whole idea behind a drill, and amateur radio runs its biggest one of the year this weekend.

The [Simulated Emergency Test](https://www.arrl.org/simulated-emergency-test), or SET, is the American Radio Relay League's annual nationwide exercise for volunteer emergency communications. The ARRL has set the focal-point weekend for October 3 and 4, 2026, though local groups may run their own on another date through the fall. Its stated purpose is plain: to let operators "test your personal emergency-operating skills and the readiness of your communications equipment," and "to find out the strengths and weaknesses of ARES, NTS, RACES and other groups in providing emergency communications."

## What is being exercised

Those acronyms are the organized side of ham radio. ARES — the Amateur Radio Emergency Service — is the volunteer program licensed operators run to support agencies during a disaster. RACES is its government-activated counterpart, and the NTS, the National Traffic System, is the standing relay network for passing written messages hand to hand across the country. SKYWARN weather spotters and Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) members take part too.

A drill is where the gaps show up cheaply. Radio that leans on ridgeline repeaters and clear line-of-sight paths — the reality in canyon country where cell coverage is thin — has a long list of parts that can quietly stop working between emergencies: a repeater knocked off the air by a lightning hit, a backup battery that no longer holds a charge, an operator who has not keyed up a formal message in a year. None of those failures announce themselves. A scheduled exercise forces each one into the open while the stakes are a logged practice message rather than a real one. The traffic even carries the word "test" before the precedence, so a scanner listener never mistakes drill messages for an actual call.

## How to take part

For a licensed operator, taking part is a matter of showing up on the right net. The ARRL's own direction is to "contact your local ARRL Emergency Coordinator or Net Manager." In these two counties the way in is the established groups that already run weekly nets — [TCARES](https://tcares.net) and the [Calaveras Amateur Radio Society](https://calaverasars.org). Both publish their net times and frequencies, and an operator who checks in will learn what their section has planned this year.

For anyone not yet licensed, the SET is a window into what organized emergency radio actually looks like, and a prompt to join it. The door is an [entry-level Technician license](http://www.arrl.org/getting-licensed), earned by passing a single 35-question written exam; it costs little and opens the local repeaters and nets. A scanner or a plain receiver is enough to listen in the meantime.

This weekend is when the volunteer radio groups find the weak link on their own terms — the dead battery, the message that takes three tries — instead of during the next real call. For a licensed operator, it is the cheapest practice of the year. For everyone else, it is a look at what readiness is actually made of.
